# 02 — Plates and manuscripts

Before screens, an explanation had to work on a page that could not move, zoom, hover or undo. Scribes, engravers and draughtsmen solved clarity with four tools: **layout** (where things sit), **keys** (letters that tie picture to words), **colour** (what it is allowed to mean) and **construction** (showing how the form is generated). This file collects their conventions from about 1000 to 1880 and turns them into rules an agent can follow on every kiruk-codex plate.

Method notes:

- Everything is summarised in our own words. Quotes are short and mostly from public-domain primary texts.
- Folio, page and plate numbers come from the holding library or cited scholarship. Where a detail comes from general knowledge and was not confirmed in a fetched source, it is marked _(unconfirmed)_.
- Each device follows the same shape: **Device** (what it is) · **Clarity** (how it helps the reader) · **Example** (one plate or folio) · **Source**.

## 1. Medieval manuscript layout

### 1.1 Pricking and ruling

- **Device.** Before any writing, the scribe measured the page layout on the first leaf of a gathering (quire), pricked holes through the whole stack at the margins, then joined the holes with ruled lines (drypoint, leadpoint or ink). This gave every page the same text block, columns and line spacing.
- **Clarity.** The grid came first and everything obeyed it. Text, gloss, initials and diagrams could share a page because each had its place on the ruling. A reader learned the grid once and could then find things on any page.
- **Example.** V&A, leaf from a Gradual, c. 1175–1200 (accession 244:1). The prickings down the right edge line up with the ruled lines.
- **Source.** V&A collections record: https://api.vam.ac.uk/v2/object/O122312 · Nottingham Manuscripts & Special Collections glossary (pricking, guide letters, catchwords): https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/researchguidance/medievalbooks/glossary.aspx

### 1.2 Rubrication (red for structure)

- **Device.** A _rubric_ (Latin _rubrica_, red earth) is text written in red: headings, incipits and explicits, liturgical instructions, running titles, paragraph marks, and the touches of red on capitals. A separate pass by the _rubricator_, often a different hand, added it after the black text was written. The scribe left space, and sometimes tiny _guide letters_, to say what went where. The reds were usually minium (red lead) or vermilion.
- **Clarity.** Red was the navigation layer, not the content. A reader could skim the reds alone and get the outline: where a section starts, what kind of text it is, what to do. Because only one extra colour was used, and used for only one job, it never competed with the text.
- **Example.** Blue and red alternate on minor initials and paraph marks across most Gothic books; red headings run through liturgical manuscripts. (A general convention, so no single folio stands for it.)
- **Source.** Nottingham glossary (rubrication, guide letter), link above. For orientation only: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubric

### 1.3 Glosses framing a central text

- **Device.** The _Glossa ordinaria_ layout (12th century onward) puts the authoritative text in a central column in a larger script. Commentary sits around it in the margins (_marginal gloss_) and between its lines (_interlinear gloss_), in a smaller script on a finer ruling. Peter Lombard's "Great Gloss" and Herbert of Bosham's edition of it pushed this furthest: several texts at once, each told apart by ruling, placement, script size, decoration and colour, and tied together by reference marks and named authorities.
- **Clarity.** The hierarchy is physical. The primary thing is centred and largest. Commentary frames it, never covers it, and sits next to the words it discusses. The reader picks the depth: text only, text plus short notes, or everything.
- **Example.** Cambridge, Trinity College MS B.5.4 (Herbert of Bosham's edition of Lombard's commentary on the Psalms, 12th century). The catalogue gives 28 lines of text against 52 lines of gloss to a full column, so the gloss runs on a ruling roughly twice as fine. Lesley Smith's study of fol. 135v counts seven texts on one page, kept apart by ruling, script hierarchy, colour and an unusually detailed referencing system.
- **Source.** Trinity College James Catalogue: https://mss-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk/Manuscript/B.5.4 · L. Smith, "Cambridge, Trinity College, MS B.5.4, folio 135v", _Manuscript and Text Cultures_ (2023): https://mtc-journal.org/index.php/mtc/article/view/9

### 1.4 Marginalia and manicules

- **Device.** Readers and correctors wrote in the margins: corrections, cross-references, _nota_ marks, and the _manicule_, a drawn pointing hand (often with a sleeved arm) aimed at one line. Margins also held drolleries and images that comment on the text.
- **Clarity.** A note lives next to what it is about, and the manicule says exactly _which line_. It was the medieval leader line and selection highlight in one mark. Because hands varied from reader to reader, a manicule also told you _who_ was pointing.
- **Example.** Leiden, Universiteitsbibliotheek, VLQ MS 99 (13th century): a large, carefully drawn hand, fingernail and all, filling much of the margin to flag a passage.
- **Source.** Erik Kwakkel, "Helping Hands on the Medieval Page" (medievalbooks.nl): https://medievalbooks.nl/2015/03/13/helping-hands-on-the-medieval-page/ · W. H. Sherman's work on the manicule, reviewed in THE: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/83253

### 1.5 Initials: decorated, inhabited, historiated

- **Device.** Initials ran in a strict hierarchy of size and finish. At the top were large painted or gilded initials opening a book or major division, then flourished red or blue initials for chapters, then plain coloured capitals and paraph marks for paragraphs. A _historiated_ initial holds a recognisable scene or person; an _inhabited_ one holds unnamed figures or animals; a _decorated_ one has ornament only.
- **Clarity.** The size of the letter tells you the level of the division, like a heading scale. The historiated initial also gives a picture of what the text is about, placed right where that text starts.
- **Example.** St Albans Psalter (Dombibliothek Hildesheim, c. 1120s–1140s), p. 109, Psalm 21: the initial shows a naked man leaping over a herd of cattle. It illustrates the line about being surrounded by calves, read through Jerome's gloss, so the picture interprets the text rather than just decorating it.
- **Source.** St Albans Psalter project (Univ. of Aberdeen / Hildesheim), "Understanding the Initials": https://www.albani-psalter.de/stalbanspsalter/english/essays/initials.shtml

### 1.6 Diagrams in scientific manuscripts: rotae, Byrhtferth, T-O maps

- **Device: rota (wheel diagram).** Concentric rings and sectors put cyclical or grouped knowledge on one circle: months, winds, zones, elements, humours, planets. Isidore's _De natura rerum_ was known as the "book of wheels" for them. Inscriptions follow the curve of each ring.
- **Device: Byrhtferth's diagram.** A harmony diagram: a circle, a lozenge and a figure-of-eight arrangement tie together the four elements, four seasons (with solstices and equinoxes), four ages of man, twelve zodiac signs and twelve winds. The letters A–D–A–M sit at the four points, from the Greek initials of the compass directions.
- **Device: T-O map.** A circle (the O, the ocean) split by a T into Asia (top, east), Europe (lower left) and Africa (lower right), often labelled with Noah's sons. It is a schema, not a survey: it shows how the world is organised, not its shape.
- **Clarity.** Geometry stands in for relationship. Things in the same ring are the same kind; things in the same sector belong together; opposites sit across the centre. The reader learns the _structure_ of a body of knowledge at a glance and then reads the labels for detail.
- **Examples.** Oxford, St John's College MS 17 (Thorney computus, early 12th century), fol. 7v: Byrhtferth's diagram. Walters Art Museum MS W.73 (English cosmological miscellany, late 12th century): twenty diagrams, most of them rotae, including winds (fol. 1v), climate zones and T-O maps. The 1472 Augsburg _Etymologiae_ (Günther Zainer) has the T-O map often called the first map in a printed book.
- **Sources.** Digital Bodleian, St John's College MS 17: https://digital.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/objects/cca30c56-0751-4f52-a952-bbffcb7b64e9/ · Glasgow ogham project on fol. 7v: https://ogham.glasgow.ac.uk/index.php/2023/01/18/byrhtferths-ogam-signature-and-oxford-st-johns-college-ms-17/ · Public Domain Review on Walters W.73: https://publicdomainreview.org/collections/cosmography-manuscript-12th-century/ · Walters detail page: https://art.thewalters.org/detail/17792 · Princeton Historic Maps, 1472 T-O map: https://maps.princeton.edu/catalog/princeton-47429f09g · Met "Pen and Parchment" (computus diagrams): https://blog.metmuseum.org/penandparchment/2009/06/30/elegant-geometry/

## 2. Villard de Honnecourt's portfolio (c. 1220s–1240s)

- **Device: geometry under figures.** About 250 pen drawings on 33 parchment leaves: buildings, machines, people, animals. On fol. 18v–19v, faces, bodies and animals are built on visible armatures of triangles, squares, circles and stars. The construction lines are left in, not erased. The drawings began in leadpoint and were then inked.
- **Device: construction as a teaching aid.** The fol. 18v inscription announces "the force of lines for drafting" as geometry teaches it ("Ci comence li force des trais de portraiture"). Zenner reads the two flamingoes on that page as a compass construction for Euclid I.1, the equilateral triangle on a given line, with the same circles giving a hexagon.
- **Device: annotation.** Short captions in Picard French sit beside the drawings and say what each one is and what it is for. A later hand (Magister II, c. 1250–60) added leaves of practical geometry, such as a man sighting a tower's height with an isosceles right triangle on fol. 20v, under a caption saying all these figures are drawn from geometry ("Totes ces figures sont estraites de geometrie", fol. 20r).
- **Clarity.** The reader sees _how to make_ the figure, not only the finished figure. The geometry works as a memory aid for a mason or carpenter: remember the triangle and you can rebuild the pose. Captions are short, next to the drawing, and in the reader's own language.
- **Example.** BnF, MS français 19093, fol. 18v (geometric figures of faces, a hand, a sheep, an eagle, a greyhound, ostriches and a horse's head).
- **Sources.** Gallica (BnF) digitisation: https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b10509412z · M.-T. Zenner, "Villard de Honnecourt and Euclidean Geometry", _Nexus Network Journal_: https://emis.muni.cz/journals/NNJ/Zenner.html · BnF Essentiels: https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/image/1a41168e-aa4e-4803-8947-358627dbd69c-villard-honnecourt-carnet-dessins-1 · Lassus facsimile (1858), Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/gri_33125015342203

## 3. Paper interaction before screens

### 3.1 Volvelles: Llull's _Ars_ and Apian's _Astronomicum Caesareum_

- **Device: Llull's combinatory figures.** Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1316) gave an "alphabet" of letters (B to K), each standing for a concept, and put them on concentric circles. In the Fourth Figure of the _Ars brevis_ (1308), the outer ring is fixed and the two inner rings turn. Each alignment reads off a three-letter "compartment", a combination of concepts to reason about. It condenses a table of 1,680 combinations into one turnable figure.
- **Device: Apian's computing volvelles.** Peter Apian's _Astronomicum Caesareum_ (Ingolstadt, 1540) is a folio of printed, hand-coloured paper dials, some with as many as five stacked rotating discs, with coloured threads as pointers. Turning the discs to values from the tables gives planetary positions and the timing of eclipses without long arithmetic.
- **Clarity.** Every possible answer is already printed on the rings. Turning a disc only _aligns_ things that exist. The reader's hands carry out the logic, so the mechanism becomes understanding, and the closed book still shows every scale.
- **Examples.** Morgan Library: the 1481 Barcelona _Ars brevis_ (Posa and Bru), catalogued with diagrams and volvelles. National Library of Scotland and Fitzwilliam Museum copies of the _Astronomicum Caesareum_ (hand-coloured; paper discs turned and read with threads).
- **Sources.** Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Ramon Llull" (orientation, with the figures): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/llull/ · Morgan Library: https://themorgan.org/node/175066 · NLS blog: https://blog.nls.uk/astronomicum-caesareum/ · Fitzwilliam conservation, "Conserving a moving book": https://conservation.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/conserving-a-moving-book/

### 3.2 Anatomical flap sheets (fugitive sheets)

- **Device.** Single broadsheets, sold cheaply from the 1530s, with a woodcut figure whose torso is covered by pasted paper flaps. Each flap lifts to show the next layer of organs. A lettered key printed around the figure explains every layer at once. Vesalius's _Epitome_ (1543) carried sheets meant to be cut out and layered the same way _(unconfirmed in a fetched source)_. Remmelin's _Catoptrum microcosmicum_ (1613/1619) took it to many-layered flap plates.
- **Clarity.** Depth becomes order. You cannot see the liver before you lift the layer above it, and that matches the order of a dissection. The key on the base sheet means nothing is lost when the flaps are down.
- **Example.** Wellcome Collection, _Interiorum corporis humani partium viva delineatio_ (Paris, Jean Ruelle, 1539): the male sheet has 5 flaps and the female 9, with an A–Z Latin key set in two columns on each side of the figure.
- **Sources.** Wellcome, 1539 sheet: https://wellcomecollection.org/works/u9kes8da · Wellcome concept page, "Anatomical fugitive sheets": https://wellcomecollection.org/concepts/ce7rratv · Wellcome, 1611 Venice copy with flaps missing (shows the base layer): https://wellcomecollection.org/works/ezt7sqs5 · McGill Osler Library on Remmelin's 1619 flaps: https://news.library.mcgill.ca/tag/flap-anatomy · Columbia, Remmelin's flap book online: https://magazine.columbia.edu/article/17th-century-flap-book-gets-new-life-online

### 3.3 Pop-ups and lift-the-flap geometry

- **Device.** Henry Billingsley's English Euclid (London, 1570, with John Dee's preface) pasted paper flaps into Book XI that fold up off the page into three-dimensional solids, glued into each copy by hand.
- **Clarity.** A flat projection of a solid is hard to read. Folding the same lines up into space proves that the flat figure and the solid are one thing.
- **Example.** Billingsley/Dee Euclid, Book XI folding models.
- **Sources.** Chetham's Library blog: https://library.chethams.com/blog/euhave-got-to-beclidding-me/ · Linda Hall Library on Billingsley: https://www.lindahall.org/henry-billingsley/

### 3.4 What paper interaction teaches

1. **The resting state is complete.** A closed flap, an unturned dial or a flat pop-up is still a full, legible figure with its key.
2. **Interaction reveals or realigns. It never adds.** Every state was drawn and printed beforehand. Nothing appears that is not already on the sheet.
3. **The affordance is drawn.** The disc edge, the hinge and the pointer thread are all visible, so the reader can see what moves before touching it.
4. **One hand for every state.** Layers are cut by the same engraver at the same scale and in the same pose, so a change of state is never a change of style.
5. **The key covers all states.** The 1539 sheet's A–Z columns explain the flaps whether they are up or down.

## 4. Vesalius, _De humani corporis fabrica_ (Basel, 1543)

- **Device: lettered keys.** Small letters are cut into the woodblock right on the structures. Each figure is followed by a printed "index of characters" (headed in the form _Decimae figurae, eiusdemque characterum index_). When the alphabet runs out, the letters double (AA–ZZ), and Greek letters and symbols follow. Paired figures share part of a key: characters common to both are marked once, and characters peculiar to one are keyed to that figure.
- **Device: sequences that peel layers.** Book II has fourteen full-page "muscle men" (eight front and six back views, pp. 170–208 in the NLM copy). The figures keep consistent poses while successive layers of muscle are removed, until the last ones are almost skeletal.
- **Device: landscape behind figures.** The muscle-men stand in a continuous landscape. Put side by side, _in reverse order_, the backgrounds join into one panorama of the Paduan countryside, a fact not noticed until the early 20th century.
- **Clarity.** The letter-and-index pair keeps the figure clean and lets the text be exact. The layer sequence turns a dissection into a readable order, and the steady pose means the eye notices only what has been taken away. The landscape tells you the figures are one series and puts the body back into the living world.
- **Example.** First muscle plate, p. 170, a frontal flayed figure in landscape (NLM Historical Anatomies).
- **Sources.** NLM Historical Anatomies on the Web, Vesalius: https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/historicalanatomies/vesalius_home.html · Univ. of Utah EHSL on the panorama and the 14 plates: https://library.med.utah.edu/blog/eccles/2025/11/19/andreas-vesalius-and-de-humani-corporis-fabrica/ · Smithsonian Libraries _Unbound_: https://blog.library.si.edu/blog/2012/10/31/not-for-the-faint-of-heart-de-humani-corporis-fabrica/ · Index pages (transcriptions to check against a facsimile): https://sourcelibrary.org/artwork/vesalius-fabrica

## 5. Agricola, _De re metallica_ (Basel, 1556)

- **Device: the lettered key system.** Capital letters sit on the parts of each woodcut, and a short key set in type right under the picture names them in reading order. Hoover's English translation (1912) keeps the form: "A—Shaft. B, C—Drift. D—Another shaft. E—Tunnel. F—Mouth of tunnel."
- **Device: cutaway mines and machines.** The ground is cut open like a cake so the shafts, drifts and tunnels show below a working surface with buildings and people. Machines have casings and walls left off. Parts are often laid on the ground in front of the assembled machine _(general observation of the plates, unconfirmed in a fetched source)_.
- **Clarity.** One woodcut shows both _where_ (the cut-open earth in context) and _what_ (letters on each part). The key under the image keeps the lookup inside one glance, and the people at work show scale and use.
- **Example.** Book V, the shaft-and-tunnel figure (Hoover ed. p. 105), keyed A to F as above. Book V's tunnel-timbering figure: "A—Posts. B—Caps. C—Sills. D—Doors. E—Lagging. F—Drains."
- **Sources.** Smithsonian Libraries, 1556 Latin edition digitised: https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/georgiiagricola00agria · Hoover translation, Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/38015/38015-h/38015-h.htm · Internet Archive (1912): https://archive.org/details/georgiusagricola00agri

## 6. Ramelli, _Le diverse et artificiose machine_ (Paris, 1588)

- **Device: cutaways.** 195 engravings, 20 of them double-page, with a description of each machine in Italian and French. Casings, walls and ground are opened to show gear trains, pumps and pipes inside.
- **Device: exploded and detached parts.** Key parts and tools are drawn again, separately, in the foreground for clarity.
- **Device: lettering.** Parts carry capital letters that the bilingual description cites _(seen in the plates; not confirmed in a fetched source)_.
- **Device: mixed viewpoints.** One engraving often combines eye-level, raised and worm's-eye views so every part can be seen.
- **Clarity.** The cutaway keeps the machine in its setting while showing how it works. The detached parts fix the problem that a working machine hides its own pieces. The "theatre of machines" staging (people, rooms, landscape) shows scale and purpose. Ramelli's plates also show the risk: the Public Domain Review notes the intricate engravings can hide as much as they show, out of Renaissance secrecy.
- **Example.** Plate CLXXXVIII, the book wheel: a rotating reading desk built like a water wheel, with the gearing that keeps each shelf level drawn in view.
- **Sources.** Science History Institute digitised copy: https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/4b29b614k · Smithsonian Libraries, "Ramelli's Machines": https://www.sil.si.edu/ondisplay/ramelli/intro.htm · Public Domain Review: https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/agostino-ramelli-theatre-of-machines/ · Internet Archive (Getty copy): https://archive.org/details/gri_33125009356607

## 7. Dürer, _Underweysung der Messung_ (Nuremberg, 1525)

- **Device: letters built with compass and ruler.** Book III builds each Roman capital inside a square, with stroke widths set as fractions of the square's side (from about a thirtieth to a tenth) and serif curves drawn from arcs of set diameters. Blackletter is built from small geometric modules. The construction is printed with the letter.
- **Device: perspective machines.** Woodcuts at the end of the book show devices for drawing in correct perspective. In the lute woodcut (fol. Q3r), a string from a fixed point on the wall passes through a frame to points on the lute. Each crossing is marked on a hinged panel, giving a dotted outline that is true by construction. The 1538 edition added more devices, such as the draughtsman with a reclining woman.
- **Clarity.** Form becomes procedure. Give the reader the square, the fraction and the arc, and the letter can be rebuilt anywhere at any size. The perspective machine shows the _method_ inside the picture, with the draughtsman drawn at work.
- **Example.** The construction of the capital A in Book III. The _Draughtsman Drawing a Lute_, 1525.
- **Sources.** Internet Archive (Getty Research Institute copy, 1525): https://archive.org/details/vnderweysungderm00drer · Columbia Libraries, "Jewels in Her Crown" (letter construction): https://exhibitions.library.columbia.edu/exhibits/show/jewels/themes/art/55 · FAMSF, _Man Drawing a Lute_: https://www.famsf.org/artworks/man-drawing-a-lute-the-draughtsman-of-the-lute · Univ. of Melbourne on rebuilding the lute device: https://blogs.unimelb.edu.au/librarycollections/2017/08/13/finding-durers-perspective/ · _Of the Just Shaping of Letters_ (Book III in English), Gutenberg: https://gutenberg.org/ebooks/37103

## 8. Hooke, _Micrographia_ (London, 1665)

- **Device: scale through familiar objects.** The book opens by magnifying things everyone knows to be sharp or fine: the point of a needle and the edge of a razor (Schem. 2, Figs. 1–2). The text compares them with printed full stops and finds them blunt and ragged. There are no scale bars. Scale comes from the reader's own sense of those objects.
- **Device: magnified detail plates.** The plates are numbered as "Schemes" (Schem. I–XXXVIII), each with numbered figures that the text cites. Hooke says he drew only after "many examinations in several lights, and in several positions to those lights", so each plate is a composite of many looks.
- **Device: foldouts.** The flea (Schem. XXXIV, facing p. 210) and the louse (Schem. XXXV) fold out to well beyond the folio page.
- **Clarity.** Starting from objects the reader knows calibrates the reader's eye. The foldout makes size itself the message: a flea bigger than the book. Numbered schemes and figures make the text a precise index into the pictures.
- **Example.** Schem. XXXIV, the flea.
- **Sources.** NLM Historical Anatomies, Hooke: https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/historicalanatomies/hooke_home.html · Science History Institute, Schem. 2 (needle and razor): https://digital.sciencehistory.org/works/th83m060n · Huntington Library: https://www.huntington.org/digital-classroom-resources/micrographia · Full text, Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/15491/15491-h/15491-h.htm

## 9. Diderot and d'Alembert, _Encyclopédie_ plates (1762–72)

- **Device: vignette over analytic plate.** A typical trade plate is split in two. The top band (_vignette_) shows the workshop in use, with workers numbered by task. The lower part (_bas de la planche_) sets out the tools, components and stages one by one on blank ground, often larger and with their own figure numbers.
- **Device: _Explication des planches_.** The words are not on the plate. A separate legend printed before each group of plates goes through the vignette and then the lower part, figure by figure and letter by letter.
- **Device: exploded and staged tools.** The lower register shows objects taken apart, in section, or as a row of stages in making.
- **Clarity.** Context and analysis each get their own space, so neither clutters the other. The scene answers _who, where and why_, and the lower register answers _what exactly_. Numbering ties both to one legend. At 2,569 plates in 11 volumes, the same template on every plate meant a reader learned how to read them once.
- **Example.** Épinglier (pin-maker), Planche I, plate volume IV (Tome 21 in ARTFL's numbering). The workshop scene sits above and the wire-working tools below. This trade is also the one Adam Smith used as his example of the division of labour.
- **Sources.** ARTFL Encyclopédie, Épinglier Pl. I (IIIF viewer): https://artfl-iiif.uchicago.edu/encyc_plate_search/encyc_viewer?https://artfl-iiif.uchicago.edu/encyclopedie/plate_manifests/V21_4_2_manifest.json&canvasID=0 · ARTFL plate search: https://artfl-iiif.uchicago.edu/encyc_plate_search/ · ARTFL home: https://encyclopedie.uchicago.edu/ · Colonial Williamsburg (example of the vignette-over-tools layout): https://emuseum.colonialwilliamsburg.org/objects/38860/brodeur

## 10. Oliver Byrne, _The First Six Books of the Elements of Euclid_ (London: Pickering, 1847)

- **Device: colour replaces letters.** Instead of "the angle ABC", Byrne prints a small coloured copy of the angle itself inline in the sentence. The diagram uses flat red, yellow and blue, plus black, and equal parts share a colour all the way through a proof.
- **Clarity.** Byrne's introduction argues that coloured parts "name themselves", hold the eye, keep the given facts in view, and let a whole class see a part when its colour is named. He claims the _Elements_ could be learned in "less than one third the time usually employed" (spelling modernised). The proof reads as a sequence of pictures, not a hunt for letters.
- **Why it matters here.** It is the bridge from old to modern. It is a pre-modern plate in a design language (flat primaries, glyphs in running text) that Bauhaus and De Stijl later made familiar, and Tufte cites it.
- **Example.** Book I, Proposition 47 (Pythagoras), where the three squares are told apart by colour.
- **Sources.** Internet Archive (Univ. of Toronto copy): https://archive.org/details/firstsixbooksofe00eucl · Internet Archive (Getty copy, full text): https://archive.org/stream/firstsixbooksofe00byrn/firstsixbooksofe00byrn_djvu.txt · Public Domain Review: https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/the-first-six-books-of-the-elements-of-euclid-1847/ · Lehigh University exhibit: https://exhibits.lib.lehigh.edu/exhibits/show/data_visualization/case_one/euclid

## 11. 19th-century patent drawings (US Patent Office)

- **Device: the 1869 rules.** Commissioner Fisher's rules (from July 1869) required thick, stiff paper; sheets 10 × 15 in., the size of the printed patent; "deep black lines" so drawings would photograph; shading in fine black lines. They banned pale or ashy tints and colours such as light blue, pink and brown, while allowing deep blue, yellow and carmine because those photograph as black. Each part had to carry the same letter or number in every view. Detached sectional views were expected, along with the inventor's signature, two witnesses, and the name at the top.
- **Device: the modern codification (37 CFR 1.84), the same tradition made explicit.**
  - **Lines:** black, uniform and well defined. Line thickness varies only where the variation has a meaning.
  - **Shading:** thin, sparse lines, with light from the upper left at 45°. No solid black.
  - **Reference characters:** numerals preferred, at least 1/8 in. high. Never on hatching or shading. One part, one character, in every view. No character in the drawing that the text omits, and none in the text missing from the drawing.
  - **Lead lines:** as short as possible, starting next to the character, never crossing.
  - **Hatching:** regular oblique lines, preferably at 45°, with a different angle for each adjacent part.
  - **Exploded views** sit in a bracket. Enlarged details are numbered as separate views.
  - **Views** are numbered "FIG. 1, 2…" in order, larger than the reference numerals. Views are not joined by projection lines or centre lines.
- **Clarity.** These rules exist so that a stranger, years later, can rebuild the invention from the sheet alone, from a reproduction, with no colour and no explanation from its author. It is the strictest one-to-one contract between words and picture in this file.
- **Example.** T. A. Edison, US Patent 223,898, Electric Lamp (1880). Fig. 1 is a section of the lamp with lowercase reference letters. Paired parts get a doubled letter ("d d" for the platinum wires), and the specification walks through them in order.
- **Sources.** 1869 rules as reprinted in _Scientific American_ (IP Mall, UNH): https://ipmall.info/node/827 · 37 CFR 1.84 (Cornell LII): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/1.84 · National Archives, Edison's lamp patent: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/thomas-edisons-patent-application-for-the-light-bulb · Specification text: https://patents.google.com/patent/US223898A/en

## 12. Colour conventions across the tradition

| Tradition                                       | Palette                                                                               | What colour means                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Gothic manuscripts                              | black text + red (minium/vermilion) + blue; gold for top-level initials               | **Structure.** Red marks headings and instructions; red and blue alternate on minor initials and paraphs; gold and painted colour mark the highest level. Colour is a level in the hierarchy, not content. |
| Computus diagrams (e.g. Byrhtferth)             | a few inks (typically red, green, brown/black) _(unconfirmed for fol. 7v)_            | **Category.** Different rings or groups get different inks so the eye can sort them.                                                                                                                       |
| Early printed books (Apian, Vesalius, Agricola) | black woodcut; red and black on title pages; hand-colouring added later               | **Copy-dependent.** Colour was painted per copy after printing, so it varies between copies and cannot carry meaning the key depends on.                                                                   |
| Encyclopédie plates                             | black engraving                                                                       | **None.** Clarity comes from line, layout and numbering alone.                                                                                                                                             |
| Byrne (1847)                                    | red, yellow, blue, black, printed flat                                                | **Identity.** Colour _is_ the part's name.                                                                                                                                                                 |
| US patents (1869–)                              | black only (deep blue, yellow and carmine allowed only because they photograph black) | **Forbidden.** It has to survive reproduction; pale tints disappear.                                                                                                                                       |

Takeaways for kiruk:

- One accent hue doing one job (the rubric) is the oldest and most durable convention.
- Colour that names parts (Byrne) is powerful, but only when printed systematically and backed by shape.
- Anything that has to survive reproduction, greyscale or a colour-blind reader carries its meaning in line and letter first.

## Rules

Each rule is a strict constraint, then _because_.

1. **Rule the sheet before the first stroke.** Set margins, the subject zone, the key and notes zones, the scale/caption band and a baseline grid. Every element sits on that grid; nothing floats. _Because_ pricking and ruling made pages predictable, and the Encyclopédie used one template on 2,569 plates. A reader who learns the grid once can find anything.
2. **The subject is centred and largest; commentary frames it and never covers it.** Notes sit in the margins beside the thing they discuss, in smaller type, tied to it by a letter or a short leader. _Because_ the glossed page let readers choose their depth (text, short gloss, full commentary) without the layers colliding.
3. **Construction stays visible, under the drawing, in the lightest weight.** Draw the geometry first (squares, circles, axes, proportions) and never erase it. _Because_ Villard and Dürer show how the form is made, so it can be rebuilt and checked. Construction that competes with the subject turns into noise.
4. **Every part the text names carries a letter, and every letter on the drawing appears in the key.** One part has one character across all views; a character never names two parts; no letter appears without a key entry. Use capitals A, B, C… for up to about 8 parts and numerals beyond that; never mix the two systems in one figure. _Because_ Agricola, Vesalius and patent rule (p) all depend on this exact one-to-one match between letters and words. One orphan letter breaks trust in all the others.
5. **Letters sit in clear space, never on hatching, shading or another line.** If a part is too small or too crowded, put the letter outside and draw a leader that starts at the letter, ends on the part, is as short as possible and never crosses another leader. _Because_ patent rules (p) and (q) and Vesalius's tiny letters in white gaps exist so a letter can be read at reproduction size.
6. **The key sits outside the drawing, in letter order, in the same letterforms, inside the same view.** Cite figures by number and part by letter ("Fig. 2, D"). _Because_ the 1539 fugitive sheet's A–Z columns and Agricola's key under the woodcut make the lookup one glance. The Encyclopédie's separate _explication_ made readers flip pages, so do not copy that part.
7. **One rubric hue, for annotation only.** Letters, leaders, headings and the modern line share one accent colour. The object itself is drawn in ink, never in the rubric. Colour is never the only carrier of meaning, so position, letter or shape repeats it. _Because_ red rubrics worked by doing one job, and the 1869 patent rules banned colour that would not survive reproduction.
8. **Colour may name parts (Byrne mode) only for 4 or fewer parts.** Each hue means exactly one part, the same swatch appears inline in the text, and each coloured part also carries its letter or a distinct fill pattern. _Because_ Byrne's colours "name themselves" and speed up a proof, but colour alone fails in greyscale, print and for colour-blind readers.
9. **To show the inside, cut; never ghost.** Use a section or cutaway with a clearly drawn cut edge, hatch the cut surfaces at 45° (a different angle for each adjacent part), and keep enough of the outside to show where the cut is. _Because_ Agricola's cut-open earth and Ramelli's opened casings keep context and interior in one view, and patent hatching rules make the cut readable.
10. **To show assembly, explode along the axis of assembly.** Parts go in order, aligned, grouped by a bracket. Draw detached parts in the foreground when the main view hides them. _Because_ Ramelli, Agricola and the Encyclopédie's lower registers solved the problem that a working machine hides its own pieces.
11. **In a sequence, hold pose, viewpoint, scale and frame fixed; change one thing per step; number the steps.** _Because_ the steady poses of Vesalius's fourteen muscle-men, and the flaps that keep their registration, mean the eye only notices what changed.
12. **Scene above, parts below, one legend.** When showing a process or tool, pair a small in-use scene (vignette) with the isolated, measured parts on clean ground, numbered in both and explained by one key. _Because_ the Encyclopédie plate keeps context (who and why) from cluttering analysis (what and how).
13. **State the scale.** Give a scale bar, a dimension, or a familiar referent drawn at the same scale. Every magnified detail is its own numbered view marked with its enlargement. _Because_ Hooke calibrated his readers with a needle point and a full stop, and patent rules require enlarged views to be labelled separately.
14. **Line weight must mean something.** Outline is heaviest, interior detail lighter, construction lightest. Shading is thin, sparse lines with light from the upper left at 45°. No solid black fills, no pale tints. _Because_ patent rules (l) and (m) and the 1869 rules ("deep black lines", no ashy tints) make form readable after any reproduction, and one consistent light source keeps shapes from being misread.
15. **Interaction only reveals or realigns what is already drawn.** The resting state is a complete figure with its key, and every state is drawn in the same hand at the same scale. _Because_ volvelles, flap sheets and pop-ups never added content, only alignment and depth. A figure that needs a click to make sense has failed as a plate.

## Conflicts

1. **Colour as annotation (rubric) vs colour as identity (Byrne).**
   - **Rubric:** one hue that means "this is navigation".
   - **Byrne:** many hues that mean "this is part X".
   - **Using both on one figure:** the reader cannot tell a red letter from a red part.
   - **Better for web:** the rubric. Use one accent for the whole annotation layer (WCAG 1.4.1 forbids colour as the only carrier of meaning). Allow Byrne mode only inside a single geometric argument with 4 or fewer parts, every colour backed by a letter or pattern, and with a rubric hue no part also uses.
2. **Letters on the part (Vesalius, Agricola) vs letters outside on leaders (patent rules for complex drawings).**
   - **Letters on the part:** keeps the eye on the object, but fails when the part is small or the figure is scaled down on a phone.
   - **Letters outside:** always legible, but adds lines.
   - **Better for web:** letters on large parts; letters outside on short leaders for anything that would fall below about 11 px at phone width. Responsive figures shrink, so this has to be decided per breakpoint.
3. **Key beside the figure (1539 sheet, Agricola, Vesalius's facing index) vs key in a separate legend (Encyclopédie _explication_, sometimes a different volume).**
   - **Better for web:** beside, inside the same viewport. Also repeat the key as real HTML text for screen readers and search. Linking letter and key on hover or focus is allowed, because it reveals what is already printed (rule 15).
4. **Letters vs numerals.**
   - **Letters:** the old masters used them, they feel right for kiruk's hand, and they run out (Vesalius went to AA–ZZ, then Greek).
   - **Numerals:** patent law prefers them; they scale without limit and carry no meaning.
   - **Better:** capitals A, B, C… for small plates (kiruk-codex already uses A–H) and numerals for anything larger. Never both in one figure.
5. **Scene behind the figure (Vesalius landscapes, Ramelli and Agricola settings) vs parts on clean ground (Encyclopédie lower register, patents).**
   - **Scene:** gives context and life.
   - **Clean ground:** gives legibility.
   - **Better for web:** the Encyclopédie split. Context goes in its own register or as a low-contrast wash, never behind letters or fine detail. Check text contrast against the real pixels behind it.
6. **Visible construction (Villard, Dürer) vs patent drawings, which ban projection and centre lines between views.**
   - **Why they differ:** one aims to teach, the other to record. A teaching plate wants the construction, and a record wants it clean.
   - **Better for kiruk:** keep construction, on its own lightest layer (the kiruk-codex `construction` layer), so it can be weighed or hidden without touching the ink.
7. **Heavy hatching and dense chiaroscuro (Vesalius, Ramelli engraving) vs thin, sparse shading (patent rules, Byrne's flat colour).**
   - **The screen problem:** dense hatching moirés and aliases at screen resolutions and blurs when scaled down.
   - **Better for web:** sparse hatching at a spacing that survives the smallest rendered size. Test at phone width and at 4× crops.
8. **Hidden layers (flaps, volvelles, foldouts) vs the single complete sheet.**
   - **The risk:** paper hid some layers, but the base sheet and key always explained them. On the web, hidden state is easily lost to no-JS, print, screenshots and search.
   - **Better for web:** the resting state is complete. Interaction only reveals or realigns, and a foldout becomes a fitted overview plus a zoomable detail, never a detail-only view.
9. **Order of a series.** Vesalius's panorama reads only when the plates run in _reverse_ order, so the reading order (dissection depth) and the landscape order disagree. On a web page, pick one order and make the other a bonus at most. Never make the reader reverse a sequence to understand it.
