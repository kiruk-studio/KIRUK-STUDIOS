Calculus of mai

Every mark on a mai plate is computed. A hand slows into each bend, a pen drains and dips, one light sets every tone, and each value is measured from the ink it labels. Change a control on any bench below: the engine redraws its specimen in your browser and charts its own numbers as it goes.

Read beside the founder’s reference, the Calculus of Interfaces.

1 · the hand

Speed falls in every bend

The hand performs the path a plate asks for. It lands, runs and leaves; it slows wherever the path bends, by the two-thirds power law of human movement; it trembles at 8 to 12 Hz; and it lifts when the line outruns its reach.

Guide

The calculation

v(s) = min( E(s), K·κ(s)−1/3 )
Speed along the path: the envelope or the power law, whichever is slower. κ is the path’s curvature, read as the turning between the chords before and after each point (up to 1.5 mm long), so a curve handed over as a polyline is read as a curve (ledger MA-27).
E(s) = v̂·[vin + (1−vin)·up(s)]·[vout + (1−vout)·down(s)]
Land, run, leave. A dip lands at 12% of the peak, a touch at 30%; a taper leaves at 70%, a lift at 30%.
K = 0.27·v̂, v̂ = pace·role·(1 − 0.35·precision)
The law’s gain comes from the hand’s pace: a contour runs at 0.55 of it, a hatch at 1.35, a letter at 0.42.
t(s) = ∫ dσ / v(σ)
Time is spent where the speed is low, so the tremor, which runs in time, shows most in the bends.
δ(t) = a·[0.62 sin 2πf1t + 0.28 sin 2πf2t + 0.25·n(38t)]
Tremor across the line, f1 in 8–12 Hz and f2 1.31–1.47 × higher, plus a slow drift that grows with length and speed.
L > 1.15·reach ⇒ lift
A free line longer than the hand’s reach (42 mm) is drawn in pieces, each landing where the last lifted.

2 · the pen

The pen drains, then dips

A dipped pen holds a little ink. Each millimetre of line spends some of it, so the line pales and thins as the pen drains; when it runs low the hand dips again and the next stroke comes out dark. That rhythm is the clock of a pen drawing.

The calculation

R ← R − (f/C)·(w/0.4)·Δs
The reservoir drains with the ink laid. C is the pen’s capacity in mm of a 0.4 mm line per dip (a quill about 240), f the ink’s flow.
λ = R0.55·(1 + 0.22·slow) − 0.1·fast
Load at the nib: a slow pen floods a little, a fast one starves.
w ← w·(0.84 + 0.24·λ)
A loaded pen lays a fuller line; a draining one thins.
D = Dink·(0.6 + 0.4·λ̄0.8)
And pales: the density of each stroke follows its mean load.
R < r ∈ [0.10, 0.26] ⇒ dip, R ∈ [0.9, 1]
The hand dips before the pen runs dry, or before a stroke it could not finish.
rpool = (w/2)·(1.05 + 1.15·P·√(rest/0.12))·(0.75 + 0.35·λ)
Where a fresh pen rests, ink gathers; capped at 0.95 mm, and at 0.7 × the line on a letter (ledger MA-16).

3 · the light

One light sets every tone

A plate declares one light. Each point of a form gets a tone from its surface normal; hatching lays the general shadow first and deepens it in two passes, and the critic checks that the ink follows the tone it was laid for.

The calculation

tone = 1 − [a + (1−a)·max(0, n·l) + b·max(0, n·lb)]
Lambert’s diffuse light over an ambient floor a = 0.18, with a bounce b = 0.14 that lifts the far side of the shadow, so its core sits at the terminator.
tone > 0.20 ⇒ hatch; > 0.50, > 0.74 ⇒ deepen
The general shadow first, then two deeper passes between its lines.
ρ = Spearman(tone, ink per cell) ≥ 0.6
The critic’s check that the ink follows the light; it also asks that the lit side stays nearly paper.

4 · the measure

A value is measured from the ink, never typed

Every number on a plate comes from the drawing. Change the part: the engine redraws it, measures the new ink, sets the chain inside and the overall a tier further out, graduates a frame from the first hole, and the critic checks each value against what it labels.

The calculation

value = round(|b − a|·k, digits)
Measured between two points of the ink at the plate’s scale k; the label is never typed.
|label − value| ≤ ½·10−digits
The critic’s measure.true, for every dimension.
tier₁ = 8–12 mm, Δtier ≥ 6 mm
Smaller dimensions inside, the overall outside, a tier apart (ISO 129; research 03 §2.2). measure.tier-gap fails under 4 mm (ledger MA-23).
gap 1.5 mm, overshoot 2.5–3 mm
Extension lines start clear of the ink and run just past the dimension line.
tick every e/k mm from the origin
A graduated neatline is a ruler laid round the figure, counted from a point of the part.

5 · the line type

A dashed line is fitted, not tiled

A line’s pattern says what it is: dashed for what is hidden, chain for an axis. The pattern is stretched to start and end on a dash, and a centre line is fitted so a dash, never a gap, lies on its middle. A hand’s dashes vary; the exact overlay’s do not.

Fit

The calculation

P = Σ pattern·d
One period, in multiples of the line width d (ISO 128): dash 12d, long dash 24d, gap 3d, dot 0.5d.
n = round((L − first)/P), even when centred
Whole periods; an even count makes the pattern symmetric, so the middle is a dash. Too short for two periods, a centre line comes out continuous, as ISO 128 allows for small circles.
k = (L − first)/(n·P)
Every element is stretched alike, so the line ends on a dash.
hand: dash × (1 + N(0, 0.16)), gap × (1 + N(0, 0.28))
A hand lifts between dashes and its gaps wander more than its dashes. The critic wants a hand’s dash CV ≥ 0.05 and an exact line’s ≤ 0.08.

7 · how the function is built

A plate is a function of its spec and its seed

Like the Calculus of Interfaces, which reads a page as UI = f(props), this page reads a plate as plate = f(spec, seed). The same spec and seed give the same marks, so every chart above is the function evaluated, not a sketch of it. Each bench is one part of f: the hand’s speed from the bend, the pen’s reservoir from the length laid, the tone from the light, a value from the ink between two points.

Where the function decides rather than varies, the decision is a threshold you can see: tone above 0.20 is hatched and deepened above 0.50 and 0.74; an empty reservoir forces a dip; an extension line shorter than 0.6 mm is not drawn. Move a control across one of those and the chart steps.

Limits

  • The numbers are real, not illustrative: they are the engine’s own per-sample trace. But they are one run at one seed; another seed moves every tremor.
  • The benches run one stroke at a time; a whole plate is only counted (section 6), not charted stroke by stroke.
  • Nothing here is differentiated or bounded. The thresholds are hard steps, and no chart claims the plate is right at sizes it was not drawn at.
  • Label contrast and the critic’s pixel checks read a raster of the plate; this page shows their results, not the raster.
